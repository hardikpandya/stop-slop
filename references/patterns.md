# Pattern Guide

Use patterns to locate weak prose, not to infer authorship. A single match is usually noise. Give more weight to repetition, co-occurrence, a sudden voice shift, or a pattern that replaces evidence.

## Content problems

| Pattern | Why it weakens prose | Better response |
|---|---|---|
| Generic significance | Claims that a topic is crucial, transformative, or complex without naming an effect | State the measurable consequence or remove the claim |
| Unsupported specificity | Precise numbers, quotations, names, or dates without a traceable source | Verify, cite, qualify, or remove |
| Vague attribution | "Experts say," "research shows," "critics argue" | Name the source and claim |
| Exhaustive tone | Broad coverage language despite thin evidence | Narrow the scope to what the draft proves |
| Repeated summary | The same point appears in the lead, section ending, and conclusion | Keep the strongest occurrence |
| Generic advice | Recommendations could apply to any company, person, or topic | Tie the advice to the case, constraint, and next action |

These are strong editing targets because they damage the article whether a person or a model wrote it.

## Structural clusters

| Pattern | Cluster threshold | Repair |
|---|---|---|
| Negative parallelism | Several "not X, but Y" or "not only X" pivots | State the positive claim; keep a contrast only when both sides matter |
| Rule of three | Repeated three-part lists or adjective trios | Keep the natural number of items and explain the important distinction |
| Template scaffolding | Repeated preview, numbered taxonomy, summary, and conclusion blocks | Remove sections that do not advance the argument |
| Mechanical balance | Every claim receives the same caveat or symmetrical counterpoint | Weight caveats by evidence and consequence |
| Uniform cadence | Many adjacent sentences or paragraphs share length and syntax | Combine, split, or reorder where meaning benefits |
| Dramatic fragments | Repeated one- or two-word sentences used as emphasis | Restore complete sentences unless the genre supports the effect |
| Meta narration | "This section explores," "it is worth noting," "here is why" | Make the claim directly |

## Lexical clusters

Words such as `delve`, `tapestry`, `intricate`, `crucial`, `pivotal`, `robust`, `landscape`, `underscore`, `foster`, `showcase`, `notably`, and sentence-initial `Additionally` have appeared often in model output. They also appear in human prose. Replace them only when several cluster together or when a plainer word carries the same meaning.

Watch for semantic habits more than a fixed blacklist:

- inflated verbs replacing `is`, `has`, `shows`, or `uses`
- abstract nouns where the draft should name an actor or event
- interchangeable praise that could describe any product or institution
- repeated transition words that turn paragraphs into a generated outline

Model preferences change. Treat any fixed word list as dated evidence, not a permanent ban.

## Citation and markup signals

Verify these before editing style:

- citations that do not resolve or do not support the adjacent claim
- plausible-looking DOI, ISBN, quotation, publication, or author combinations
- leftover chat instructions, knowledge-cutoff disclaimers, or refusal language
- fake quotation marks around a paraphrase
- unexplained Markdown headings, bold labels, or list scaffolding copied into prose

## Voice-shift test

When the writer supplied other text, compare the draft with that baseline:

- vocabulary and idiom
- sentence and paragraph length distribution
- punctuation and contractions
- tolerance for uncertainty
- use of examples, anecdotes, and technical detail

A sharp unexplained shift deserves review. It still does not prove AI authorship.

## Weak cues

Do not edit these in isolation:

- one em dash or semicolon
- one passive sentence
- one adverb or transition
- a Wh- sentence opening
- first-person pronouns or contractions
- a three-item list
- correct grammar or consistent spelling

These can be the right choice for the sentence and genre.
